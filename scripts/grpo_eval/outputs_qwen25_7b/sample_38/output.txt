To determine John's running speed, we need to calculate the distance he runs each day and then find his average speed over the week.

First, let's break down the distances:
- On the first day, John runs for 3 hours.
- On the other two days, he runs for half as long as the first day, which is \( \frac{3}{2} = 1.5 \) hours each day.

Next, we need to know his speed. Speed is calculated using the formula:
\[ \text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}} \]

Let's denote John's speed by \( v \) (in miles per hour).

### Day 1:
- Distance: \( 60 \times \frac{1}{7} = \frac{60}{7} \) miles (since he runs 60 miles in total over 7 days)
- Time: 3 hours

Using the speed formula:
\[ v = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}} = \frac{\frac{60}{7}}{3} = \frac{60}{7 \times 3} = \frac{60}{21} = \frac{20}{7} \approx 2.857 \text{ miles per hour} \]

### Day 2 and Day 3:
- Distance: \( 60 \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{120}{7} \) miles (since the remaining 2 days account for the rest of the 60 miles)
- Time: 1.5 hours each day

Using the speed formula for each of these days:
\[ v = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}} = \frac{\frac{120}{7}}{1.5} = \frac{120}{7 \times 1.5} = \frac{120}{10.5} = \frac{1200}{105} = \frac{240}{21} = \frac{80}{7} \approx 11.429 \text{ miles per hour} \]

Since the speed should be consistent, we need to check if there was an error in the initial assumption. Let's re-evaluate the total distance covered in terms of speed:

If John runs 60 miles in total over 7 days, and he runs 3 hours on the first day and 1.5 hours on the other two days, the total time is:
\[ 3 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 6 \text{ hours} \]

The average speed is:
\[ v = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} = \frac{60}{6} = 10 \text{ miles per hour} \]
